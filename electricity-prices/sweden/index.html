Electricity in Sweden costs 2.99 kr per kilowatt-hour — $0.31 once converted — which puts it +3 % above the median of the 23 countries tracked on this site. That is the price with all taxes included, for a household in the 2,500–4,999 kWh a year band — one definition applied to every country, because mixing tax-inclusive and tax-exclusive figures is what puts most country comparisons out by 15% or more.

What that means in practice: a household using 3,700 kWh a year pays about 11,063 kr for the electricity itself. The number on your own bill will differ — that is what the retailer table below is for.

Heating in Sweden: gas or a heat pump?

Gas costs 2.309 kr per kWh in Sweden, against 2.99 kr for electricity — so electricity is about 1.3 times the price of gas here. All taxes included on both sides, same period, so they can be compared directly.

That ratio decides how a house is cheapest to heat. A heat pump delivers several units of heat per unit of electricity; a 92% condensing boiler delivers slightly less heat than the gas it burns. The two cost the same to run at a seasonal COP of 1.2 — that is the break-even for Sweden. That is below what almost any modern air-source heat pump manages over a season, so in Sweden the heat pump is the cheaper machine to run and it is not close.

How clean the electricity is

Price is only half of what a kilowatt-hour costs. The grid in Sweden emits 45 grams of CO₂-equivalent per kWh on a lifecycle basis, averaged over a year of generation. For the same 3,700 kWh household that is about 167 kg of CO₂ a year.

For scale: the cleanest grid tracked here is Norway at 20 g/kWh, the dirtiest is South Africa at 950 g/kWh. Sweden sits in the cleaner third, 3rd cleanest of 23.

This matters for a practical reason as well as an environmental one: on a clean grid, swapping a gas boiler for a heat pump cuts emissions immediately. On a coal-heavy one, the same swap mostly moves the emissions from your street to the power station.
