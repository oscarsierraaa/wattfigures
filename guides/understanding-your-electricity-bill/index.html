How to Understand Your Electricity Bill

By Oscar S. · Updated: 2026-07-18

Decode your electricity bill line by line: the unit rate, standing charge, taxes and what actually drives the total you pay each month.

Estimates, not bills: these figures use typical wattages and the price per kWh you enter. Your real cost depends on your tariff, your usage and your specific hardware. Check your bill or meter for exact numbers.

An electricity bill can feel like it is written to be confusing, but almost every one is built from the same handful of parts. Once you can see them, you can tell at a glance whether a bill is high because you used more, because prices rose, or simply because it is an estimate. This guide walks through each line and shows you what actually moves the total.

The two things you pay for

Nearly every bill splits into two charges. The unit rate is the price of the energy you actually use, quoted per kilowatt-hour (kWh) — one kilowatt of power running for one hour. The standing charge (or fixed charge) is a flat daily fee for being connected to the grid, and you pay it even in a month you use almost nothing. A bill that looks high in a low-usage month is often mostly standing charge.

Your energy cost is simply kWh used × unit rate. That is the same maths every tool on this site uses — you can see it in action in the electricity cost calculator, which turns any appliance's wattage and hours into the same kWh figure your meter records.

Reading the numbers on the page

Most bills show a start and end meter reading, the difference between them (your consumption in kWh), the unit rate applied, and the standing charge for the number of days in the period. Multiply consumption by the unit rate, add the standing charge across the days, add tax, and you have the total. If the figures do not line up, the usual culprit is an estimated reading.

Why estimated bills catch people out

If your supplier has no recent meter reading, it estimates your usage from past patterns. Estimates can run high or low, and they eventually correct when a real reading arrives — which is why a "surprise" catch-up bill often follows a run of estimates. Submitting your own reading, or having a smart meter that reports automatically, replaces guesswork with the actual number and keeps bills honest.

What actually drives your total

Three levers move a bill: the unit rate you are on, how many kWh you use, and the fixed standing charge. You cannot do much about the standing charge day to day, and the unit rate is set by your tariff, so usage is where your own habits show up. A handful of appliances dominate most homes: heating and cooling, water heating, and anything that runs constantly like a refrigerator or freezer. Knowing which of your devices are cheap and which are expensive is the whole point of this site.

Turn the bill into action

Once you understand the bill, take the unit rate printed on it and use it everywhere. Put it into any calculator here and the results will match your real tariff instead of a generic default. If you are on a variable or time-of-use tariff, use the rate for the hours an appliance actually runs. From there you can compare devices, spot the expensive ones, and see the effect of small changes before you make them — which is far more useful than simply being surprised each month.

Frequently asked questions

What is the unit rate on my bill?

It is the price you pay per kilowatt-hour (kWh) of electricity used. Multiply it by your consumption in kWh to get the energy part of the bill.

What is a standing charge?

A fixed daily fee for being connected to the grid, charged whether or not you use any electricity. It is separate from the unit rate for energy used.

Why is my bill an estimate?

If the supplier has no recent meter reading it estimates usage from past patterns. Submitting a reading, or having a smart meter, replaces estimates with actual figures.
