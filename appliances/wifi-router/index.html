It runs continuously, 24 hours a day. Energy and cost at $0.18/kWh, the US average.

A Wi-Fi router is a small appliance with an outsized presence on the bill, not because it uses much power at any moment, but because it is one of the very few things in a home that genuinely never switches off. Most routers draw somewhere between 5 and 18 watts continuously, all day and all night, all year. This page treats it as an always-on device, so the yearly figure captures the reality that a router runs 8,760 hours a year whether anyone is using the internet or not.

Individually the cost is small — a typical 10 W router costs only a handful of dollars a year — but it is a useful example of how constant low-power devices work. The same watt drawn continuously for a year uses far more energy than a high-power appliance used briefly, which is exactly why standby and always-on loads deserve attention across a whole home. A router, a mesh satellite or two, a modem and a network switch together can quietly add up.

There is not much to optimise on the router itself, and you generally would not want to — turning it off saves only pennies and breaks smart-home devices, security cameras and anyone working from home. The practical takeaway is comparative: if a 10 W router costs so little to run non-stop, it puts the cost of larger always-on appliances like fridges and freezers into perspective, and shows why switching off genuinely idle electronics matters more than unplugging the internet.

Home internet is usually two boxes, not one, and the second is easy to forget. The router does the Wi-Fi and the routing; in front of it there is often a separate modem, an ONT for fibre, or a cable box from the provider, and each draws its own 5 to 10 W. Both run every hour of the year, so the pair is what actually appears on the bill. A mesh system multiplies it again — each satellite is another router, drawing much the same as the main one.

How to cut Wi-Fi router running costs

  • Leave it on — the saving from switching a router off is tiny and breaks smart devices.
  • Do turn off old routers, modems or switches you no longer use and forgot to unplug.
  • Group genuinely idle electronics, not the router, on switched strips for real savings.
  • Replacing several old separate boxes with one efficient modem-router can trim a few watts.
  • Use the router's cost as a yardstick for other always-on devices around the home.

Frequently asked questions

How much does it cost to leave a router on all year?

A 10 W router running non-stop uses about 88 kWh a year, roughly $16 at $0.18/kWh. Lower-power models cost less. It is small, but it runs every hour of the year.

Should I turn my router off at night to save money?

It rarely makes sense. The saving is a few dollars a year at most, and it disrupts updates, smart-home devices, cameras and anyone online late. Focus on genuinely idle electronics instead.

Why does such a low-power device matter at all?

Because it never switches off. It shows how a small constant draw becomes real energy over 8,760 hours, the same reason fridges and standby loads add up across a home.

How much power do a modem and router use together?

A router is typically 10 W and a separate modem or fibre ONT another 5 to 10 W, so the pair is around 15 to 20 W running continuously. That comes to roughly 131 to 175 kWh a year, about $24 to $32 at $0.18/kWh. If your provider supplies a single combined unit, expect it near the top of the router range rather than the sum of both.

How many watts is a Wi-Fi router?

Between 5 and 18 W, with about 10 W typical. Older units and ones with many active antennas sit at the top of that range; a simple modern router is nearer the bottom. The number barely moves with how much you are downloading — a router at idle and a router streaming draw close to the same, because the radios are on either way.
