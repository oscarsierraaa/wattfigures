A desktop PC uses far more electricity than a laptop doing the same tasks, because it is built for performance rather than battery life. A basic office desktop might draw 80 to 150 watts, a typical machine around 200 watts, and a powerful gaming or workstation PC 400 to 600 watts or more under load — before you add the monitor. This page uses an hours-per-day approach; enter a realistic figure for how your machine is actually used, since the draw swings widely between idle browsing and heavy gaming or rendering.

The key detail is that power varies enormously with the workload. The same PC that sips 90 watts while you write emails can pull five times that while gaming, so the honest yearly cost depends on how you use it, not just how long it is on. Leaving a desktop running around the clock — a common habit — is where the real waste hides, because even at idle it draws far more than a sleeping laptop. Sleep and hibernate settings, and pairing it with an efficient monitor, are where the savings are.

If your machine is a gaming or workstation build, the graphics card is usually the biggest single draw, spiking only under heavy load. For everyday tasks it idles far lower, so the yearly cost is really a story of two numbers: a modest idle figure most of the time, and short, expensive bursts when you genuinely push it hard.

For context, a typical laptop doing the same office tasks draws roughly 30-65 W — a fraction of even a modest desktop's 150-200 W. Over a year of daily use, that gap alone is often worth real money at typical prices, before counting the desktop's separate monitor. That is not a reason to avoid desktops — performance per watt for demanding work still favours them — but it is worth knowing the running-cost premium you pay for a machine that is always the more powerful option.

The single number that decides a desktop's running cost is the graphics card, and it only matters when the machine is actually working. An office PC with integrated graphics idles at 50 to 80 W and rarely leaves that range; the same box with a mid-range GPU sits at a similar idle but climbs to 300 W or more the moment a game or a render starts. That is why the power supply rating on the side of the case is no help at all — an 850 W unit does not draw 850 W, it is a ceiling. What you pay for is the average over the hours the machine is on, which for most people is much closer to idle than to peak.

How to cut desktop PC running costs

  • Set the PC to sleep or hibernate when idle instead of leaving it running.
  • Don't leave it on overnight for downloads unless you really need to.
  • Enable power-saving modes; they cut idle draw with no real downside for office work.
  • A laptop does everyday tasks for a fraction of a desktop's power — consider it for light use.
  • Undervolting or an efficient power supply helps on high-end gaming machines.

Frequently asked questions

How much does a desktop PC cost to run?

At 200 W for two and a half hours a day, about 183 kWh a year — roughly $33 at $0.18/kWh, plus the monitor. A gaming PC used heavily can cost several times more; a basic office PC less.

Is a desktop much more expensive than a laptop?

Yes, usually several times more for the same tasks. Desktops are built for power, not efficiency, so for everyday work a laptop costs far less to run over a year.

Should I turn my PC off or leave it on?

For anything beyond a short break, sleep or shut down. A desktop left running all day draws far more than a sleeping one, and modern PCs wake in seconds, so leaving it on rarely pays.

How much electricity does a desktop computer use per hour?

A typical desktop at 200 W uses 0.2 kWh an hour, about $0.036 at $0.18/kWh. An office machine idling at 80 W is nearer 0.08 kWh, and a gaming PC under load at 500 W is 0.5 kWh — roughly $0.09 an hour. The spread between those three is far wider than the difference any settings change will make.

Does the power supply rating tell me what my PC costs to run?

No, and it is the most common way people overestimate. A power supply's wattage is the maximum it can deliver, not what the computer draws — a 750 W unit in a machine that idles at 70 W delivers 70 W. The only way to know your own figure is a plug-in meter, or adding up the idle and load draw of the parts you actually have.
